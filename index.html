Welcome aboard.
You're in for a ride.

Everything's called something different on a boat. Right is starboard, left is port, and it just keeps going from there — you'll pick it up. Ask if there's something you can't translate. Anything can fall in the water — keep that in mind when you set your things down.

Solar panels, aft
☀️ Power

We make our own power

We always keep the 12V running, so lights, internet, and so on just work, no fuss. The stove and power outlets need the inverter switched on though: we check that the voltage is above 13V, flip the switch for the inverter, and boom — power! The inverter's usually on while the sun's shining and we've got power to spare. Once the sun clocks out, we ration our usage and switch it off when we're done.

Water

We make our own water

We make our own water — both for everyday use and for drinking — straight from the sea. The desalination system sits right next to the anchor chain. We've got plenty of lovely water, but it takes power and time to make, so we use it thoughtfully.

Dishes

Sometimes it pays off to rinse things in the sea first — saves water, and lets one person rinse while another washes up.

Sea bath

A popular personal-hygiene move around here: jump in the water, soap up, jump back in, then rinse off in the shower on the bottom step.

The galley
Cooking

The bottom-left burner is running a little more "chill" than the manufacturer intended, but you've only got three pots to work with anyway, so no biggie. Enjoy cooking. And if it fits your plans, maybe prep some food while the sun's out and we've got power to spare.

Fridges

We have 4 fridges — the extra one doubles as a chair around the table. It's either a fridge and a freezer or only freezer.

We have most fruit and veg on the fridge — that's how it keeps longest.

Dry stores

There's canned goods, rice and pasta in the three floor hatches, which you open with the red suction cup. There's also a stock of food in the coffee table. Dry goods that are open we keep in the cupboard to the left of the stove. Remember to pack things well. Enjoy.

House rules
  • Know the footwear rule – wear non-marking soled shoes
  • Know the rule: one hand for the boat, one for yourself – hold on when the boat moves
Rain

When it rains: close all windows. We might as well be quick about it, sometimes it pours in a matter of seconds.

Checklist

Setting sail

  • All hatches are closed — and locked
  • The dinghy is hauled all the way up, and tied down — on both sides
  • The bucket is tied down
  • The aft sea railing is closed, both sides
  • The ladder is tied down
  • Anything that can slide is secured
  • Turn on "navigation instruments" on the control panel
Reference

Knots

Figure-eight knot

Danish: 8-tal / ottetalsknob

A stopper knot tied near the end of a line, so it can’t run out through a block, fairlead, or car.

Watch on YouTube ↗

Slip knot

Danish: slipstik

Used to fasten something quickly, in a way that’s just as quick to untie again — often tied in the middle of a line as well as at the end.

Watch on YouTube ↗

Clove hitch

Danish: dobbelt halvstik

Used to tie something — a fender, say — around a rail or post. Quick to tie, and the height is easy to adjust up or down.

Watch on YouTube ↗

Bowline

Danish: pælestik

Forms a fixed loop at the end of a line, so that end can be fastened to something — the boom, a ring, a post. Stays easy to untie even after being loaded hard.
